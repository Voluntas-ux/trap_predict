the short version

Check whether the machine is random before you trust anything built on top of it.

One round of watching the whole squad — 150 targets — is enough to tell. Write down which way each target went, feed it to the checker, and you get one of three answers: the machine looks clean, something is off, or it is plainly following a pattern.

If it is clean, counting beats everything else. Keeping track of what you have already had at a station gets you right about 6 times in 10. Nothing can do better. That is a mathematical fact, not a gap waiting for better technology.

If it is not clean, the checker names the pattern and says what it is worth — typically a jump from 6 in 10 to somewhere between 7 and 9 in 10, depending on how crude the pattern is.

Either way, you cannot build a reliable target predictor. Six separate reasons, set out below. Anyone promising otherwise is selling something.

why this question comes up at all

The setup, the snag, and the question

What is true

At each station, every athlete gets two left targets, two right targets and one straight, across five visits. Which order they arrive in is decided by the machine's controller, and nobody on the line is told.

What is not guaranteed

Nothing published says how the controller generates that order, or how anyone would check it. Randomness is an assumption. If a controller has a quirk, the athletes who spot it gain an edge over the ones who do not.

So the question is

Is this machine actually drawing at random — and if it is not, how much is the pattern worth to someone who notices? Both parts are answerable from data anyone at a range can collect.

point one
1

Counting already gets you 6 in 10. That is the number anything else has to beat.

Each station hands out a fixed allocation: two lefts, two rights, one straight. So if you have had a left and a straight already, only one left and two rights remain, and the odds have moved. Keep count and the guesses get better as the station empties out. The fifth target is not a guess at all — it is whatever is left.

This matters because it is where most claims go wrong. A system that predicts 70 per cent of targets sounds impressive against a coin toss and is barely worth having against counting. Whenever someone quotes a hit rate, ask what they are comparing it to.

point two
2

If the machine has a pattern, one round is usually enough to catch it and name it.

The trick is to watch the whole squad, not just yourself. Your own 25 targets are a thin slice. All six athletes across all five stations is 150 targets in the same round, which is plenty. Five patterns get tested, each one something a controller could plausibly be doing:

Everyone gets the same order

All six athletes at a station receive the identical sequence. Crude, and instantly obvious once you look across the line.

The same order, shifted along

One sequence per station, each athlete starting at a different point in it. Looks perfectly random if you only watch yourself.

Never the same twice running

The machine refuses to throw the same direction back to back.

Each athlete repeats themselves

One athlete gets the same order at all five stations. Nothing looks wrong at any single station.

Avoiding the last direction thrown

The controller steers away from whatever the machine group just threw, in shooting order.

The checker reports how strongly each fits, and the strength adds up as you record more rounds at the same range. Here is what that buys you when a pattern really is present:

One pattern deserves its own mention: a controller that works from a fixed set of prepared rounds. Each round it produces looks perfectly random — no test of a single round can ever catch it. The giveaway is a whole round turning up a second time, and two identical rounds of 150 targets do not happen by chance. Once you have logged the set, you know the rest of a round from its opening targets. The checker watches for this automatically, and it is the main reason to keep recording after the first round.

point three
3

And still, a reliable prediction system cannot be built. Six reasons.

A clean machine leaves nothing to find

If the draws really are random, 60.7 per cent is the ceiling and no method gets past it. Not more data, not a bigger model. It is a proven limit, not an engineering problem.

A slight tilt is invisible in practice

Real controllers fail gently, not completely. A machine applying a pattern to a quarter of its draws takes four to nine rounds at the same range to detect, and buys one or two percentage points — lost inside the normal variation of a day's shooting.

Left, straight and right is not where to hold

Knowing the direction says nothing about the angle, the height or the speed. Those come from the machine settings, which vary by machine and drift across a session as springs warm and tire. A direction is not an aiming point.

Nothing stays the same

Settings change between rounds and between days. Controllers differ by make and software. A pattern found at one range in one session is not evidence about the next range, or even the next morning.

The best case is still a probability

Even after identifying a pattern with certainty, the best subtle case reaches about 77 per cent — because the pattern itself has to be learnt from the squad's early targets. The only route to near-certainty is a repeated round, which may simply not be happening.

And the information arrives late

Accuracy climbs through the round: the opening targets are close to a coin toss, and the gains land in the last third, by which point most of the round is gone. Shooting first at a station is shooting with the least information.

This is why the honest deliverable is a measurement, not a prediction tool. The useful output is a number: how much predictive information this machine leaked, in percentage points above 60.7, at this range, on this day. That number is worth having. A promise to call the next target is not.

What was checked, and what was wrong

An earlier version of this test already existed. It was re-run from scratch, at its own settings and then at a different random seed with twenty-five times more simulation. The results held up: it is a correct test, correctly calibrated. Five things around it needed fixing.

A right number for a wrong reason

One quantity was justified by an argument that does not hold — the parts of a round it treats as unrelated are in fact linked. The number itself was right, and is now derived properly.

The wrong culprit named

When the test fires, it points at which of its six checks was responsible. In the strongest cases that finger-pointing turns out to be an artefact of how the test is run, and it changes if you simulate more. Do not act on it.

A table with no script behind it

One published table could not be regenerated and had to be reverse-engineered to confirm. The project's own rules require every table to come from a script; this one now does.

An overstated headline

"Catches everything" is true of the five things it was tested against, one of which no real controller would do. It misses prepared-round reuse entirely, and is weak against shooting-order effects.

practical

What to actually do

  1. Record a full round. Every target, every athlete, every station: which way it went. Miss some and the check still works, it just has less to go on. Note the range, the date and the session, because a result belongs to those.
  2. Run it through the checker. It gives a verdict, the pattern that fits best, how strong the evidence is, and how many percentage points above counting the pattern would be worth.
  3. If nothing shows, record three or four more rounds before concluding anything. A quiet first round is weak evidence, not a clean bill of health. The checker tells you how far along you are.
  4. If something shows, keep recording at the same range. Evidence accumulates. Watch particularly for a whole round repeating: that one is decisive on its own.
  5. Report the measurement, not a prediction. "This machine leaked nine points above counting across six rounds on 14 March" is a claim you can defend. "I can call the next target" is not.
  6. Keep it off the line. This is for training review and equipment checks, after the fact. It is not built for use during competition, and should not be used that way.
