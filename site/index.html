one squad · five stations · 150 targets

What generated this round, and can anything beat counting?

A trap release controller is supposed to hand every athlete two lefts, two rights and one straight at each station, in an unpredictable order. This is an audit of that claim. It watches one round of 150 targets, scores seven candidate controller algorithms against each other, and predicts the next target shot by shot — then reports honestly how much of that survives contact with reality.

33.3%blind guess
60.7%optimal counting
93.4%best case identified

The middle number is the ceiling if the controller is clean. The right-hand number is what the model reaches when it is not, and section nine is about why the gap between them is narrower in practice than it looks here.

Every visit to a station throws one of three direction classes. Across five visits an athlete gets two lefts, two rights and one straight. The order is the whole question.

the unit of observation

A round is not 25 numbers. It is a 6 × 5 × 5 array.

Six athletes rotate through five stations, visiting each station five times. Every target is fired in view of the whole line, so one round yields a complete array of 150 labelled targets — thirty rows, each row a permutation of {L, L, S, R, R}. There are exactly 30 such permutations.

That matters because the instinct that one round is too small comes from counting a single athlete's 25 shots and ignoring the other 125. The squad is the observation unit.

The round also has a firing order, not just a shape. Shot n is athlete n mod 6 at station n mod 5 on visit ⌊n/30⌋. Some controller behaviours only exist in that order, and a test built on the array alone cannot see them.

Because each station hands out exactly two lefts, two rights and one straight, an athlete who counts what they have already had at that station beats chance without any model at all. That counting predictor reaches 60.67 per cent over a full round, and the fifth visit to any station is always certain. Every number on this page is measured against it.

what it is good for · 01 · saying which algorithm

Identification, not just rejection

A significance test returns one bit: reject or do not reject. That bit is not actionable, because the next question is always "so what do I build instead?". The model library answers with a posterior over seven named controller algorithms and a Bayes factor for each, which is graded, has no floor, and needs no tie-break.

Each family below is a real mechanism a controller could implement. λ is contamination strength: λ = 1 is a pure scheme, λ = 0 is a clean controller, and everything in between is a controller that applies the scheme to some of its draws.

A1 · shared ordering

Every athlete at a station gets the same order. The crudest possible dependence, and the easiest to catch.

A2 · master plus offset

One ordering per station, each athlete receives a cyclic rotation of it. Invisible to a marginal frequency check.

A4 · no back-to-back repeat

A within-row constraint. Twelve of the thirty orderings survive it, which is why it also thins the orbit space.

A5 · cross-station coupling

An athlete's five station rows are copies of one draw. Nothing at a single station looks wrong.

A7 · firing-order avoidance

Sequential: the controller avoids repeating the direction just thrown by the same group. Only visible in firing order.

LIB · scheme reuse

A fixed library of whole-round schemes, drawn round after round. Undetectable inside any single round. See section six.

At full strength the posterior lands on the right family with probability 1.00 and log Bayes factors of 26 to 84 — odds of billions to one. At half strength it reports graded evidence of 3 to 16 where the battery's rejection rate swings between 14 and 100 per cent. At quarter strength it says, correctly, that it does not know.

The clean row is the one to read twice. Starting from a prior of 0.50, a clean round moves the posterior on "clean" only to 0.84. One round cannot certify a controller. It can only fail to convict one.

what it is good for · 02 · predicting the next target

Prediction, scored the only way that counts

The same object that identifies the controller also predicts. After every pull, the posterior predictive is a distribution over the next target: a weighted mixture across all twenty-one models in the library. Score it prequentially — each prediction made before seeing the outcome, then averaged — and compare against counting. That is a test no amount of model misspecification can flatter.

accuracy over a full round · model against counting

Grey is the counting baseline, amber is the mixture. Whole-round accuracy from 80 simulated rounds each; the dashed line is the counting ceiling of 60.7 per cent.

Insurance is nearly free

On a clean controller the mixture costs 0.003 nats per shot and 0.001 in accuracy against pure counting. Carrying the whole library when there is nothing to find is not a real cost.

Learning is fast when there is something to learn

Against a master-plus-offset controller the model is at 91 per cent by the third block of thirty targets and 100 per cent from the fourth — identified from the squad's shots before your own turn comes round.

Not knowing which algorithm barely hurts

The mixture stays within 0.02 in accuracy of an oracle told the true model and the true λ. Almost all the gain comes from knowing that some structure exists.

what it is good for · 03 · knowing when to stop collecting

How many rounds you actually need

The predecessor design answered this with a power table: fix a number of rounds, simulate, read off a rejection rate, and repeat the whole study whenever the design changes. Likelihoods make it a one-line calculation instead. Evidence adds across rounds at the same range, so the expected log Bayes factor per round is the information rate, and a stopping rule follows: keep collecting until the cumulative evidence crosses a threshold in either direction.

Expected log Bayes factor per round for the best-matching library member — in every case the member at the true λ — against a clean controller, from 80 simulated rounds each. ln BF = 5 is about 150 to 1.
truthln BF / roundsdrounds to 150:1
evidence ladder · cumulative ln BF against rounds collected

Straight lines because evidence is additive. A controller applying a scheme to half its draws is convicted inside one round. At a quarter strength it takes between four and nine rounds at the same range, and the clean case drifts steadily the other way.

This is also the honest form of a negative result. A single clean round is not evidence of a clean controller; it is roughly half a nat of evidence. Any report of "no dependence found" has to state the evidence rate alongside it, or it is quoting the absence of a search.
what it is good for · 04 · the thing no single-round test can see

Scheme reuse across rounds

Suppose a controller holds a library of K fixed whole-round schemes and draws one per round. Inside any single round the output is exactly what a clean controller produces — identical distribution, no statistic can tell them apart. The six-statistic battery rejects such rounds 5.4 per cent of the time, which is precisely its own false-positive rate. It is blind by construction.

Across rounds it is the loudest signal in the whole problem. An exact repeat of a 150-target round carries a log Bayes factor of 30·ln30 − ln K, about 99 for a twenty-scheme library: odds of 1043 to one from a single coincidence. And once a repeat is recognised from its opening targets, the remaining targets of that round are known exactly.

The registry predictor is deliberately crude: match the round so far against every scheme already recorded, and if exactly one is consistent, read the rest off it; otherwise fall back to counting. Its accuracy is jagged by design — a round drawn from a scheme already seen is predicted almost perfectly, a new one falls back to 60 per cent — and how fast the average climbs is entirely a function of K against how many rounds you have watched. Push K to sixty and the same forty rounds leave you closer to the baseline than to certainty. Matching should also be run up to athlete relabelling and rotation of station slots, which multiplies the number of comparisons but changes nothing about the method.

what the audit found

Five corrections

The implementation is correct and the numbers are reproducible. The reasoning around them needed work in five places, ordered here by consequence.

Columns are not independent

3.1 · structural claim

The design note argues that one round supplies twenty-five independent columns of six athletes. Six athletes within a column are indeed independent. The five columns at the same station are not: they are built from the same six rows, and every row is a permutation, so what appears at one visit constrains the others. The exact covariance between two visit-columns' chi-squares is 1/6, giving a null variance of 625/6 and a standard deviation of 10.206 rather than the 9.354 independence would imply.

Nothing breaks, because p-values come from simulation rather than a chi-square table. But the rationale has to be reworded to "five independent station blocks, within which athletes are independent and visit-columns are not", and no analytic shortcut may be used for that statistic: a chi-square reference would understate the spread by eight per cent and be anticonservative in exactly the tail that matters.

why the columns are tied together

Attribution is an artefact of the p-value floor

3.2 · procedure

The note reports that one alternative splits its "driving statistic" between two of the six, and explains it by a real structural overlap. The overlap is real, but the split is not about sensitivity. Under that alternative one statistic is identically zero — a value never seen in the null table — while the other sits below the null minimum. Both p-values are therefore pinned at their floors, and the tie is broken by whether each statistic was declared one-sided or two-sided, which costs a factor of two. Change the null table from 8,000 rows to 20,000 and the attribution flips from 46/47 to 22/77. The step that says "read the driving statistic and adopt the corresponding model" must not run on floored p-values.

The pooling table had no script

3.3 · reproducibility

Every table in the project is supposed to be regenerable by a single script, with no hand-typed numbers. The pooling table was not. It has been reproduced here by inferring the method, and the method should be added to the tool as a flag.

"Power 1.000 against all alternatives" over-claims

3.4 · framing

True, and also a statement about five alternatives chosen by the same author who chose the statistics. One of them is a rejection-sampling construction rather than anything a controller would plausibly implement. The design note is careful about this; the summary that reached the work plan is not. Two plausible alternatives against which the battery is weak or blind are quantified in section six and in the limitations below.

Smaller things

3.5 · assorted

The min-p calibration is documented as leave-one-out but includes each row in its own reference set, an O(1/n) discrepancy that the large calibration check shows is harmless. One statistic is a strict subset of another and adds little unless opening-slot effects are specifically suspected. A remark about needing a fifteen-symbol alphabet for machine-level analysis is superfluous, since within a station machine role and direction class are the same thing. And the data protocol needs to record firing order, because the alternatives that matter most only exist in it.

the honest part

Why a precise prediction model cannot be built

Everything above is real and reproducible. None of it adds up to a system that tells an athlete which machine is about to fire. The obstacles are not engineering gaps that more data or a bigger model would close; several of them are structural, and one of them is a theorem. This section exists so the results above are never quoted without it.

If the controller is clean there is nothing to find

01 · a ceiling, not a benchmark

Under independent uniform draws the five station urns factorise: no information passes between stations, no athlete's targets say anything about any other's, and the best possible predictor is the one that counts what it has already seen. That ceiling is 60.7 per cent and it is a theorem, not a state of the art. A model that reaches it has added exactly nothing, and on a clean controller the whole apparatus on this page performs 0.1 percentage points worse than counting in your head, because it pays a small price for entertaining hypotheses that turn out to be false.

Weak contamination is effectively invisible

02 · the regime that matters most

The realistic failure of a controller is not a pure scheme, it is a slight tilt. At a quarter strength the evidence rate is 0.57 to 1.4 nats per round, so distinguishing that controller from a clean one needs four to nine rounds at the same range under the same settings — and prediction gains at that level are 1 to 2 percentage points, which is inside the noise of a single athlete's day. Worse, pooling helps more slowly than intuition suggests: one alternative sits at the size of the test at k = 1 and does not reliably improve through k = 5.

The library is finite, and reality is not

03 · unmodelled alternatives

Seven families are implemented. A controller could implement something none of them captures — a linear congruential generator with a short period, a balanced incomplete block design, a hand-written schedule with human quirks — in which case power is unknown rather than high. The prequential score partly protects against this, since a wrong model that predicts better than counting is still evidence of structure, but "we tested the alternatives we thought of" is the honest description of every result on this page.

Direction class is not a trajectory

04 · the prediction is coarse

Everything here predicts one of three labels. A left target can be anything across a wide fan of angles, at a range of heights, with speed varying by machine and by how recently its spring was set. Knowing "left" does not tell you where to hold. Converting a direction prediction into an aiming decision requires the per-machine calibration, the ballistic model and the vision pipeline — all of which are separate unvalidated projects, each with its own error budget that compounds on top of this one.

Nothing here is stationary

05 · the model does not transfer

Machine settings change between rounds and between days. Spring tension decays across a session, so the same machine throws differently in the afternoon. Controllers differ by manufacturer and firmware. Whatever is learned at one range under one configuration has no guarantee of holding at the next, which means the evidence clock resets far more often than the "rounds needed" table implies, and cross-range validation — not cross-round — is the number that would matter.

Ground truth is expensive and video is not a substitute

06 · the data problem

Reliable labels come from a controller log, which requires cooperation from a range. Coding from video yields direction class at best, never machine identity, and needs a coder agreement threshold demonstrated on held-out footage before any of it can be used. Irregular targets, repeats and malfunctions all have to be reconciled against the firing order by hand. There is no scraping route to a large clean dataset here.

Even perfect identification leaves you probabilistic

07 · the best case is not certainty

Identify a master-plus-offset controller with certainty and the accuracy still only reaches 76.8 per cent over the round, because the master ordering itself is unknown until enough of the squad has fired. The only path to near-certainty is an exact repeat of a whole round from a scheme library — a specific, checkable, and quite possibly absent behaviour. Structure bounds the uncertainty; it does not remove it.

Information arrives after it is useful

08 · the timing problem

The model's accuracy climbs across the round: block 1 is near chance, block 4 is where the gains sit. An athlete shooting first at a station has the least information; by the time the posterior is sharp, most of the round is over. Combining the prior with a vision system does not fix this either, since a prediction has to arrive inside the break window, and coupling the two means a failure of the independence assumption silently corrupts the vision results too.

Analytic freedom is the largest single risk

09 · the researcher, not the model

Statistics chosen after seeing data lose their calibration; a library extended after a rejection is no longer a fair test; a holdout split at random rather than by range, scheme and session will report a number that does not survive contact with a new range. With this many degrees of freedom, an unregistered analysis will produce an inflated result with near certainty. Pre-registration and label-shuffle checks are not ceremony here, they are the load-bearing controls.

And the intended use is offline

10 · scope

This is built as an audit and training-analysis instrument: a measurement of how much predictive information a given release controller leaks, in accuracy points above 60.7 per cent, computed after the fact from a log. It is not designed for, and should not be used as, an aid during competition. The value that scales is the audit itself — a fixed data schema, an identification engine, a predictability score and a registry of scheme fingerprints per range — which makes controller randomness a measurable property rather than an assumption.

Real data

A round is a CSV with one row per athlete-station and columns athlete, station, v1…v5, entries L, S or R. For the Bayesian tool, pass a 6 × 5 × 5 integer array with 0 = L, 1 = S, 2 = R to run_round, together with the firing order. Rows that are not permutations of {L, L, S, R, R} fail loudly rather than being tested, because a violation means the null is misspecified rather than merely false.

Simulation study only. No real controller data has been analysed here, and no claim is made about any particular range, manufacturer or event. Every figure on this page comes from the scripts in the repository, with seeds recorded.
